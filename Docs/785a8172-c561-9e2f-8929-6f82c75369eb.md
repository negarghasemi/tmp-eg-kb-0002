## Purpose of this Reference Guide

This guidebook addresses typical compressed air systems in small and medium manufacturing facilities, covering common design and operating problems. It provides guidance for optimizing compressed air systems for peak performance, especially in situations like rising utility bills, replacing old compressors, or suffering production due to insufficient pressure.

### Why Compressed Air?

Compressed air is expensive to produce and likely consumes a significant portion of energy costs.

**If you are like most industrial manufacturing or processing businesses, chances are that you have a compressed air system. Your air compressor system may be located somewhere out of sight and out of mind at your facility. You may not know what it costs to operate and maintain.**

Compressed air is a controllable cost, and this guidebook helps identify ways to reduce energy, maintenance, and capital costs associated with operating compressed air systems.

### What this Guidebook is and is Not

This guide helps understand compressed air costs and reduce them through energy efficiency best practices. It is general in nature and not a design guide for new systems. It does not replace equipment manuals or maintenance procedures. **Users are cautioned to follow proper health, safety, and lockout procedures.**

## What is Compressed Air?

Compressed air is a form of stored energy used to operate machinery, equipment, or processes. It is common in manufacturing and service industries where electrical energy may be impractical or hazardous.

*Figure 1  Conversion of Atmospheric Air into Compressed Air*

A typical air compressor converts approximately 7 volumes of atmospheric air into 1 volume of compressed air at about 100 psig (7 bar), releasing heat and moisture. Useful energy is released when this compressed air expands back to atmospheric pressure.

*Recovered heat from the air compressor can potentially be used as an energy efficiency measure for other processes, such as space and water heating.*

### Compressed Air Costs

Compressed air is expensive to produce. Over the first ten years of a typical air-cooled compressor's life (two-shift operation), operating costs (electricity and maintenance) account for **88%** of the total lifetime cost, with **electricity being 76%**.

*As energy accounts for about 76% of the overall lifetime operating cost, it is very important to design and purchase the most efficient components for your compressed air system. It is recommended to make purchase decisions on the overall expected lifetime operating costs, and NOT just on the initial cost of the equipment.*

## Introduction to Compressed Air Systems

Compressed air systems consist of **Supply** (compressors, air treatment, primary storage) and **Demand** (piping, secondary storage, end-use equipment) sides.

**As a rule of thumb, for every horsepower (HP) in the nameplate capacity, the air compressor will produce approximately 4 standard cubic feet per minute (scfm).**

*Figure 5 - Common Air Compressor System Components (Diagram shows air compressors, aftercoolers, \"wet\" receiver, air dryer, filter, \"dry\" receiver, pressure controller, air distribution pipes, and end use equipment.)*

### Compressed Air Use

**In many facilities, compressed air systems are the least energy efficient of all equipment. There is a tremendous potential to implement compressed air energy efficiency practices.**

Best practice calls for decisions to be based on the life cycle cost of the compressed air system and components.

*Properly managing a compressed air system can not only save electricity, but also decrease downtime, increase productivity, reduce maintenance, and improve product quality.*

For a typical compressed air end use, it takes about **10 units of electrical energy input** to the compressor to produce about **one unit of actual mechanical output**.

## Air Compressor Types and Controls

Two basic types: **Positive Displacement** (rotary screw, vane, reciprocating) and **Dynamic** (centrifugal, axial).

### Rotary Screw Compressors

Common in sizes from 5 to 900 HP. Available as lubricated or dry (oil-free) types.

**The biggest advantage of screw compressors over small air cooled reciprocating units is that they can run at full load continuously where the reciprocating compressors must be used at 60% duty cycle or below. Rotary screws are also a lot quieter and produce cooler air that is easier to dry. Be aware that rotary screw compressors may not be the most efficient choice compared to start/stop reciprocating compressors.**

### Reciprocating Compressors

Commonly 1 HP to 30 HP, often used for building control and automation systems.

**Large reciprocating compressors still exist in industry, but they are now no longer commercially available, except for use in specialized processes such as high pressure applications.**

### Vane Compressors

Used for smaller applications where floor space is an issue, but less efficient than rotary screw compressors.

### Compressor Motors

Electric motors power compressors. For new or replacement units, **premium high-efficiency motors** should be specified due to quick payback from energy savings.

### Compressor Controls and System Performance

Efficient flow control at part loads is essential.

*Consideration should be placed to both compressor AND system control selection as they are important factors affecting system performance and energy efficiency.*

**Individual Compressor Control Strategies:**

-   **Start/Stop**: Simplest and most efficient. Motor turns on/off based on discharge pressure. Appropriate for compressors < 30 HP.
    **Repeated starts may cause the motor to overheat and place greater maintenance demands on compressor components. For this reason, care should be taken in sizing storage receivers and maintaining wide working pressure bands to keep motor starts within allowable limits.**
-   **Load/Unload**: Motor runs continuously, but compressor unloads when pressure is adequate. Unloaded rotary screw compressors typically consume **15-35% of full load power** with no useful output. Optional unload timers can save energy.
    **Load/unload control strategies require significant control storage receiver capacity for efficient part load operation.**
    *Figure 7 - Average Power vs. Capacity for Rotary Screw Compressor (Shows power consumption at various capacities for Load/Unload with 1 gal/cfm and 10 gal/cfm receivers.)*
-   **Modulating Control**: Varies compressor output by adjusting the inlet valve. Even at zero flow, modulating rotary screw compressors typically consume about **70% of full load power**. Least efficient for these units.
    **Compressor controls have a significant impact on energy consumption, especially at lower flows, where start/stop controls are generally the most energy efficient.**
    *Figure 8 - Rotary Screw Compressor with Inlet Modulation Control (Shows power consumption at various capacities for Inlet valve modulation with and without blowdown.)*
-   **Variable Displacement**: Uses special control valves to vary output capacity. Good efficiency above 60% loading.
    *Figure 9 - Rotary Screw Compressor with Variable Displacement (Shows power consumption at various capacities.)*
-   **Variable Speed Drive (VSD)**: Varies compressor speed to match air demand. Most efficient at part loads.
    **In most cases a variable speed drive compressors offers the most efficient part load operation. Ideally, when there are multiple air compressors at a facility. One or more fixed speed compressors would supply the base load compressed air, and a VSD compressor would be used to supply the fluctuating or trim load.**
    *Figure 10 - Variable Speed Rotary Screw Power Curve (Shows power consumption at various capacities with and without stopping.)*
    *To benefit from VSD compressors, the appropriate amount of air receiver storage volume needs to be evaluated for different flow and control scenarios.*
    **Variable speed drive (VSD) compressors should be considered for trim (or swing) duty as they are typically the most efficient unit to supply partial loads. Capable of supplying a constant pressure through a wide control range, the energy consumption and flow of a VSD compressor is almost directly proportional to the speed. This can result in energy savings over comparable fixed speed units when compressors are partially loaded. Be aware, however, that at full loads, the VSD will use slightly more energy compared to a similar sized constant speed motor drive.**

### Multiple Compressor System Controls

Goal: Maintain the lowest and most constant pressure through all flow conditions, with most compressors at full load or off. The trim unit should be efficient at partial loads.

**Proper coordination is required to maintain adequate system pressures and increased efficiency whenever more than one compressor is required to run in a compressed air system.**

-   **Cascaded Pressure Band Control**: Simplest, but results in higher-than-necessary system pressures during partial loads, increasing energy consumption.
-   **Network Control**: Compressors communicate to optimize stop/start, load/unload, modulate, and vary speed. Typically manufacturer-specific.
-   **System Master Controls**: Interface with local controllers, can accommodate different manufacturers and types.
-   **Multiple Controls with Variable Speed Drives (VSD)**: VSDs for trim duty. Ensure VSD capacity is equal to or larger than the largest fixed speed compressor to avoid control gaps.

**To benefit from multiple compressor control, the appropriate amount of air receiver storage volume needs to be installed to slow system pressure changes and allow time to start and stop compressors. Storage is most important for load/unload control but is also required for systems using VSD compressors.**

## Compressed Air Auxiliary Equipment

Includes aftercoolers, filters, separators, dryers, heat recovery, lubricators, pressure regulators, air receivers, condensate drains, and automatic drains.

### Air Compressor Coolers

*Approximately 80 percent of the electrical energy going to a compressor is converted into heat. As an energy efficiency option, this heat of compression can be recovered and used to produce hot water or hot air. Compressed air heat recovery provides an excellent opportunity for energy efficiency at many facilities.*

-   **Intercoolers**: Remove heat between compression stages. Clean periodically for maximum heat transfer.
-   **Aftercoolers**: Remove heat from lubricant and discharge air. Proper operation is crucial for moisture content. Clean periodically.
-   **Compressor Intake**: **Air should be as cool as possible for maximum energy efficiency** (cold air is denser). Ingesting outdoor air improves efficiency.
    *By ingesting an outdoor air intake supply (as opposed to air from a very warm compressor room), the energy efficiency is improved. When designing outdoor air intakes, pressure differential, freezing, and ice blockage in winter conditions need to be evaluated to maximize energy savings. Moreover, compressor rooms should be as clean and cool as possible to provide the foundation for optimal compressor operation.*

### Air Dryers

Remove moisture to prevent corrosion and contamination. Dew point suppression is crucial for certain applications.

*The typical pressure drop across a compressed air dryer is 3 to 5 psi. Some dryers found in industry are undersized and cause even higher pressure drops. For ongoing energy efficiency, compressed air should be dried only to the dew point required, and with the appropriately sized dryer.*

**Refrigerant Dryers:**

-   **Non-cycling Refrigerant**: Low initial cost. Suitable for dew points > 2C. Consume near full power even when lightly loaded due to hot gas bypass valve.
    *Energy can be saved by turning off the air dryer during the hours when the air compressor is off.*
-   **Cycling Refrigerant**: Same dew point as non-cycling, but uses a thermal mass, allowing on/off operation and good part-load efficiency.
-   **Variable Speed Refrigerant**: Uses electronic means to match drying capacity to demand, offering good part-load efficiency.

**Regenerative Dryers (Desiccant Dryers):** Capable of very low dew points (-40F or C). Purge air requirement can be a major energy penalty.

*Dewpoint controls are available that can match the regeneration energy requirement to air system demand. Dewpoint controllers should be used for energy efficiency improvements.*

-   **Heatless Regenerative**: Uses already dried compressed air for regeneration (15-20% of nameplate rating constantly). Costs ~3-4 kW per 100 cfm.
-   **Exhaust Purge Regenerative**: Uses smaller amount (7%) of heated dried air. Costs ~2.25 kW per 100 cfm.
-   **Blower Purge Regenerative**: Uses heated ambient air, no compressed air is used for regeneration. Costs ~2.5 kW per 100 cfm.

**Membrane Dryers:** Use a semi-permeable membrane. Have no moving parts. Use about 20% of nameplate rating for sweep air, which is lost.

### Filters

-   **Compressor Air Inlet Filter**: Protects compressor. Replace periodically. **High inlet filter pressure differential reduces capacity and efficiency.**
-   **Compressed Air Filters**: Remove contaminants downstream. Finer filters lead to higher pressure differential.
    **Generally the finer the filter, the more pressure differential exists across the element. Particulate filters are used to remove solid particles and have the lowest differential.**
    *About **1% in higher energy costs** results from every **2 psi** in filter differential. If a given filter capacity is doubled the pressure loss across it will reduce by a factor of 4, for a 75% savings. From an energy efficiency perspective, air filter types should be chosen carefully as there is an energy penalty for over filtering.*
    *To save energy, where possible, minimize the filter pressure drop by using low differential mist eliminator style filters, oversized filters, or by using filters installed in parallel.*
    *Example 1 - Cost of Clogged Filters: A 100 HP compressor operating for 2 shifts costs $32,330/year. If it operates at 110 psi to overcome a 10 psi filter differential, the energy penalty is **5% or $1,615 per year**.*

### Receivers and Air Storage

Adequate storage improves air quality, stability, and efficiency. Crucial for screw compressors.

-   **Primary Air Receivers**: General system storage, near compressors. Dampen pulsations, allow condensate to settle, supply peak demands, reduce load/unload cycles, slow pressure changes.
    **As a rule of thumb, for load/unload operated lubricated screw compressors, the receiver volume should be 5 to 10 US gallons (20-40 liters) per trim compressor scfm output.**
    *Locating primary receiver downstream of the dryer and filters is best.*
-   **Secondary Air Receivers**: Located in distribution system or at end use. Protect sensitive end uses from pressure dips, protect multiple end uses from large transient users, stabilize pressure in undersized piping.
    *Example 2 - Determining Size of Air Receiver: For a clamp using 2 cfm needing 85 psi for 2 minutes from a 100 psi system, a **29 US gallon** receiver is required.*
    **Facilities having large fluctuations in air demand, or having insufficient air pressure (usually at the end of the line), should evaluate the need for one or more air receivers strategically located in the air distribution system.**

### Separators and Drains

-   **Water Separators**: Remove entrained liquids after aftercoolers.
-   **Drains**: Remove condensate from separators, filters, dryers, and receivers. Failed drains cause moisture problems and waste air. Replacing a manual drain consuming 5 cfm in a two-shift operation saves about **$425 per year**.
-   **Main Drain Types**: Zero air loss traps (most efficient), electrically operated solenoid valves (can waste air), float operated mechanical drains (prone to blockage), manual drains (should be avoided due to constant air escape).

### Piping

Piping delivers compressed air. Undersized piping causes pressure loss, increasing compressor energy by about **1% for every 2 psi increase**.

-   Horizontal piping should be sloped with moisture drainage.
-   Add 30% to expected airflow when designing for future expansion.
-   Looping distribution piping can reduce pressure differential by **75%**.
    *To minimize energy loss from pressure differential and to help stabilize the end of line air pressures, the distribution system should be sized for no more than **2-3 psi** pressure differential.*
-   Consider lower friction materials (copper, extruded aluminum) and larger diameter pipes.
    *If a pipe area is doubled, for a given flow, there is generally one quarter the pressure drop for the single diameter pipe - a **75% energy savings**.*
    *For every **2 psi pressure drop** caused by undersized or bottlenecked piping, there is approximately **1% increased energy required**.*
    *In order to minimize pressure drops, where possible, elbow and angle joints should be smooth and gradual.*

### Flow Controllers

Stabilize system pressure more precisely than compressor controls. Can achieve more efficient lower average facility pressure.

*Savings of about **1% per psi reduction** can be gained due to lower pressure if a significant portion of the load is unregulated. This savings effect is minimal if the majority of the end uses are regulated.*

-   Flow controllers isolate compressors from downstream storage. Adequate storage on the compressor side is crucial.
-   There is an energy penalty for running the compressor discharge pressure higher upstream of the flow control.

### Filter Regulator Lubricator Devices (FRLs)

Pressure regulation at end use, often combined with filter and lubricator.

-   Filters should be sized for minimal pressure differential.
-   Precision regulators with large diaphragms reduce pressure droop.
-   Lubricators ensure proper tool lubrication, improving efficiency.

### Fittings

Fittings and couplings need to be durable and airtight. Poor quality clamps and quick coupling fittings can cause significant pressure drops.

**Quick coupling fittings can be a major point of pressure drop. Often the air must flow through more than one coupling to feed the end use, with each coupling causing up to 7 psi pressure differential at its rated flow. It is not uncommon to see pressure differentials of up to 30 psi across the hoses and couplings. This can be addressed by upsizing connectors and hoses for minimal pressure differential.**

## Uses and Misuses of Compressed Air

Compressed air is used for diverse applications. Its cost is often unknown, leading to inefficient use.

*For many applications, other sources of power may be more cost effective and energy efficient. Typically, less than **10% of the original energy** used to produce compressed air is actually converted into useful work by the end use application. Think about the equipment at your facility and ask if any of the end uses can be converted to other power sources.*

### Inappropriate Uses of Compressed Air

Applications where other energy sources would be more efficient or economical.

*Although one of the most expensive forms of plant energy, compressed air is easily accessible, and simple to adapt for use. Consequently it is commonly used for applications where other energy technologies and energy inputs would be more efficient and economical.*

## So You Want to Perform a Compressed Air System Assessment?

Evaluating your system is the first step. Assessments can be done in-house or by qualified consultants. Focus on a systems approach, examining individual components and their interactions.

*A qualified practitioner can assist you in performing the assessment and making system improvement recommendations. Contact your utility for a list of qualified and experienced firms in your area. Many provincial and municipal electric distribution companies offer technical support for these assessments. Financial aid may be available from federal and provincial governments and local utilities to defray part of the cost of doing a system assessment and implementing capital improvements.*

**Common steps in an improvement program:**

1.  Gather equipment nameplate data, receiver volumes, piping sizes, and lengths; create system drawing.
2.  Establish baseline by measuring current performance.
3.  Establish required performance levels (pressure, power, air quality).
4.  Analyze data, review operating history, calculate costs to identify improvement areas.
5.  Assess alternatives and improvements for technical and economic options.
6.  Determine best options for sub-components.
7.  Devise an implementation plan.
8.  Examine maintenance and purchasing practices.
9.  Factor in current deficiencies and future requirements.
10. Question if there's a better way to supply and use compressed air.

*Compressed air needs are defined by the air quality, quantity, and level of pressure required by the end uses in your facility. Analyzing needs carefully will ensure that a compressed air system is configured properly. The higher the quality, the more the air costs to produce. Higher quality air usually requires supplementary equipment, which not only increases original capital investment, but also makes the overall system more expensive to operate in terms of energy consumption and maintenance costs.*

### Establishing a Baseline

Measure parameters like air pressure, pressure differentials, compressor Amps/kW vs. time, system flow, temperatures, and calculated operating costs. Identify leaks, inappropriate uses, and assess filtration, storage, and dryers.

*Figure 25 - Example of Compressed Air Pressure and Motor Amp Profile.*

### Analyzing Performance Data and Establishing Performance Levels

Analyze data to identify deficiencies and improvement opportunities.

*Optimizing peak compressed air performance requires an examination of the relationship and interaction of components acting as a system, plus an independent component evaluation.*

**Areas to evaluate:**

-   **Compressor type, size, and condition**: Appropriateness, overall condition, installation, air intake, ventilation, heat recovery.
-   **Primary and Secondary Receivers**: Location and size effectiveness.
-   **Compressor Controls**: Appropriate pressure set points, adjustment of pressure bands for multiple compressors.
-   **Filters**: Cleanliness, appropriateness, pressure drops, maintenance schedules.
-   **Aftercooler and moisture separator**: Efficiency, cooling effectiveness.
-   **Dryer**: Appropriateness based on end use, size, pressure drops, efficiency.
-   **Automatic Drains**: Location, condition, effectiveness, energy-efficient alternatives.

### Devising a Plan

Calculate energy savings based on alternatives:

-   Identify equipment for shutdown.
-   Select and use compressor and flow controllers.
-   Opportunities to downsize or purchase new equipment.
-   Minimize compressed air equipment operating hours.
-   Proper selection of air compressors (stages, type, control modes).

*When selecting new or replacement equipment for your compressed air system, remember to base your decision on the overall expected life cycle operating cost. Avoid purchasing decisions made on initial price alone. Obtain **Compressed Air Gas Institute (CAGI)** compressor performance specifications to compare the full load performance of one brand versus another. In addition to providing you with unbiased advice, your local utility can often suggest the names of qualified practitioners in your area to help you with your compressed air plans.*

## Proven Energy Efficiency measures

### Compressed Air System Leaks

Leaks can be a significant source of wasted energy (20-30% of compressor output is not unusual). Proactive management can reduce leaks to <10%.

*Experience has shown, time after time, that fixing air leaks is most often the top priority for any compressed air system optimization. Typically you will find that your efforts will have a simple payback of less than 6 months.*

**Indifference to air leak management can lead to purchasing unnecessary air compressor capacity, thereby increasing capital expenses.**

*Figure 26 - Annual Cost of Compressed Air Leaks Based on $0.10/kWh Electricity Cost*

| Leak size | 1 Shift (2250 hrs) | 2 Shifts (4250 hrs) | 3 Shifts (8400 hrs) |
| :-------- | :----------------- | :------------------ | :------------------ |
| 1/16\" leak | $200               | $380                | $750                |
| 1/4\" leak  | $3,210             | $6,070              | $11,990             |
| 3/8\" leak  | $7,230             | $13,650             | $26,980             |
| 1/2\" leak  | $12,820            | $24,210             | $47,850             |

**The type of compressor control can have a large effect on the results of any leak reduction effort. For example, a leak reduction effort that reduces the air consumption by 10%, in a system with a single modulating compressor, would only achieve about 3% in energy savings because of the limited turndown capability of the modulating compressor control. This same reduction when applied to a system with a VSD compressor would result in energy savings of about 10%.**

**How to Track Down Air Leaks:**

-   Perform a low-load test during non-production time.
-   Use ultrasonic leak detectors in noisy environments. Soapy water for precise location.
-   **Best time to find leaks: when the plant is not operating (night/weekends).**

**Preventing Air Leaks:**

-   Install fittings properly with sealants.
-   Isolate non-operating equipment with valves.
-   Lower system air pressure (reduces flow rate, not a cure).
-   Select high-quality fittings, hoses, tubing, disconnects.

**Remember that once leaks have been repaired, the compressor control system often needs to be adjusted so as to achieve the true energy savings potential.**

### Lower Compressor Discharge Pressure by Minimizing Pressure Drops

High discharge pressure increases energy consumption. Often set artificially high to overcome system pressure drops.

*The higher the discharge pressure from the air compressor, the more money and electricity it costs to produce the compressed air. A rule of thumb for systems in the 100 psig range is: for every 2 psi increase in discharge pressure, energy consumption will increase by approximately 1 percent at full output flow.*

**In general, a properly designed compressed air system should have a pressure loss (or drop) of much less than 10 percent of the compressor's discharge pressure, measured from the point of discharge to the point of end use.**

**Ways to Minimize Pressure Drops:**

-   Select equipment with lowest practical pressure drop (aftercoolers, filters, dryers).
-   Maintain air filtering and drying equipment.
-   Properly design distribution system (pipe diameter, looped configurations).
-   Reduce air travel distance.
-   *Minimize energy loss: size distribution system for no more than **2-3 psi** pressure differential.*
-   Assess end-use pressure requirements; minimize system pressure accordingly.
-   Check air pressure at tool inlet. Address large pressure drops from undersized lines, quick couplers, FRLs, hoses.
-   Specify regulators, lubricators, hoses, and connections with lowest pressure differential.
-   Use larger size couplings (e.g., 3/8\" coupler has 1/6th the pressure differential of 1/4\" coupler at same flow).

### Minimizing Compressed Air End Use Energy Requirements

-   Replace inappropriate applications (e.g., open blowing) with efficient models (vortex nozzles, atomizers).
-   Install a flow controller to lower plant pressure and reduce artificial demand.
-   Turn off air-consuming equipment when not in use.
-   Avoid operating air tools without a load.
-   Replace worn tools.
-   Lubricate air tools as recommended.
-   Group end-use air equipment with similar requirements.

### Air Compressor Heat Recovery

*Approximately **80 percent of the electrical energy** used by an industrial air compressor is converted into heat. For many facilities, a properly designed heat recovery unit can recover **50-90 percent** of this available thermal energy to offset space and water heating.*

**Heating Air:**

-   Packaged rotary screw compressors are ideal. Ducting and HVAC fans can extract heat from aftercooler and lubricant cooler.
-   Approximately **50,000 BTU per hour** of heat can be extracted for each 100 scfm air at full load.
-   **Heat recovery ventilation duct must not restrict compressor cooling air flow. Booster fans may be required.**

**Heating Water:**

-   Waste heat from lubricant coolers can produce hot water for central heating, boiler systems, industrial cleaning, etc.
-   Typically, **50-60 percent of compressor heat** can be recovered for water heating.
-   **Heat recovery strategy must not restrict compressor cooling water flow to prevent overheating.**

### Implement More Efficient Compressor Control

Significant power savings are possible by running compressors in more efficient modes.

-   For multiple compressors, use advanced controls (network, master controls) for optimal energy and operational performance.
-   Compressors need time to start; consider extra storage capacity.
-   Adjust cascaded pressure bands as needed.
-   The \"trim compressor\" should be efficient at partial loads.

### Install Storage Capacity

Receivers improve efficiency and stabilize system pressures.

-   Locate receivers as close to compressors as possible.
-   For load/unload rotary screw compressors, install **10 US gallons per cfm** of compressor capacity.
-   Protect against freezing in condensate drains for receivers exposed to subfreezing temperatures.
-   Select slightly larger receivers than currently required.
-   Consider two receivers (before and after dryer) when air needs to be dried.

### Optimize Air Dryers

Dryers consume significant compressed air or electrical power.

-   For new purchases of refrigerated air dryers, choose energy-saving cycling styles.
-   Avoid drying air to a dew point lower than required.
-   Use energy-saving dew point controllers for all regenerative desiccant dryers.

### Reduce System Drainage

Condensate drains are a common point of compressed air loss.

-   Consider airless drains as replacements for timer or manual drains.
-   Procure drains with a gauge glass for visual malfunction indication.
-   Regularly test automatic drain traps.
-   Slope piping downwards and away from compressors; locate drains at main header bottoms.
-   Avoid open manual drain valves.

### Purchase a More Efficient Compressor

-   Purchase the most energy-efficient compressors, including those with premium efficiency motors.
-   Operate base load units at maximum capacity.
-   Consider purchasing at least one Variable Speed Drive compressor for flow variations above base load.
-   A two-stage compressor might provide better system efficiency as a base compressor.

## Maintaining Your Compressed Air System

Maintain all equipment according to manufacturer specifications.

-   Leaks increase operating costs and interfere with controls.
-   Plugged coolers cause moisture problems.
-   Oil separator differential increases power consumption.

*From an energy efficiency perspective, in many cases, it is wise to maintain equipment more frequently than the recommended intervals. This is especially true for managing air leaks, high pressures, moisture and controls.*

## Before and After Case Examples

### Case 1: Install Purge Controller, Repair Leaks and Lower Pressure

*A furniture manufacturer with two 100 HP modulating screw compressors (1,000 cfm dryer, 6,000 hrs/year) had high energy consumption and pressure issues.*

**Before:**

-   **Total compressed air production**: 450 cfm
-   **Total compressor energy consumption**: 880,000 kWh
-   **System specific power**: 32.9 kW/100 cfm
-   **Total electrical cost**: $88,000/year (at $0.10/kWh)
-   **Air dryer purge**: 150 cfm
-   **Leaks**: 100 cfm
-   **Drains**: 5 cfm
-   **Average compressor discharge pressure**: 120 psi
-   **Dryer and filter pressure differential**: 10 psi
-   **End use piping differential**: 20 psi
-   **Required end use pressure**: 80 psi

**After (Implemented Recommendations):**

-   **Leak reduction**: 50 cfm
-   **Drainage reduction**: 5 cfm
-   **Plant pressure reduction**: 15 psi (10% flow reduction = 25 cfm)
-   **Dryer purge reduction**: 117 cfm
-   **Total flow reduction**: 197 cfm
-   **Final average compressor loading**: 253 cfm
-   **One compressor turned off, remaining unit consumes**: 456,000 kWh/year
-   **Savings with existing modulating control**: **$42,400 per year (48%)**

**Further measures recommended (VSD compressor, reduced differentials):**

-   **New system electrical consumption**: 300,000 kWh
-   **New system specific power**: 19.8 kW/100 cfm
-   **New system electrical cost**: **$30,000 per year**
-   **Total Savings**: **66%**

### Case 2: Use Smaller Compressor During Off-Hours

*The same company installed a dry fire system requiring 5 cfm during weekends when no production occurs.*

**Before:**

-   **100 HP modulating compressor running for 2,760 extra hours/year to supply 260 cfm (dryer purge, leaks, drains, fire system)**.
-   **Power required**: 76 kW
-   **Total power per year**: 209,760 kWh
-   **Operating cost**: **$20,976 per year**

**After:**

-   **Small 5 HP (4.25 kW) 15 cfm reciprocating compressor used only for the fire system (isolated with check valve)**.
-   **Operating consumption**: 3,910 kWh
-   **Annual cost**: **$391**
-   **Annual savings**: **$20,585 (98%)**

### Case 3: On/Off vs. Load/No Load Control

*A small automobile dealership considering upgrading its 15 HP reciprocating compressor (50 cfm @ 175 psi) to a new 15 HP screw compressor.*

**Existing System (Reciprocating):**

-   **Compressor consumption**: 9,737 kWh/year ($974/year)
-   **Dryer consumption**: 3,296 kWh/year ($330/year)
-   **Total system cost**: **$1,304/year**

**Proposed System (New Screw Compressor, Load/Unload, Standard Dryer):**

-   **Compressor consumption**: 30,335 kWh/year ($3,034/year)
-   **Dryer consumption**: 3,296 kWh/year ($330/year)
-   **Total system costs**: **$3,364/year**
-   *The new screw compressor costs about **2.5 times more** in electricity.*

**Alternative High Efficiency System (On/Off, Optimized):**

-   **Measures Applied**: Reduced pressure differential, new cycling dryer (0.51 kW), average discharge pressure reduced to 115 psi, compressor shuts off immediately, airless drain, added storage receiver, pressure regulating valve for constant 100 psi shop pressure.
-   **Proposed compressor consumption**: 4,455 kWh/year ($446/year)
-   **Dryer consumption**: 140 kWh/year ($14/year)
-   **Total system costs**: **$460/year**
-   *The efficient system consumes about **35% of the original system** and **14% of the load/unload system**.*

## Glossary

-   **Absolute Pressure**: Total pressure measured from zero (perfect vacuum).
-   **Actual cubic feet per minute (acfm)**: Flow rate of air at actual operating pressure and temperature.
-   **Aftercooler**: Heat exchanger for cooling air discharged from a compressor.
-   **Atmospheric Pressure**: Measured ambient pressure for a specific location and altitude.
-   **Automatic Sequencer**: Device that operates compressors in sequence.
-   **Brake Horsepower**: Horsepower delivered to the output shaft of a motor or engine.
-   **Capacity**: Amount of air flow delivered under specific conditions (cfm).
-   **Check Valve**: Valve permitting flow in only one direction.
-   **Compression Ratio**: Ratio of absolute discharge pressure to absolute inlet pressure.
-   **Cubic Feet per Minute (cfm)**: Volumetric air flow rate per minute converted back to ambient conditions.
-   **Cycle**: Series of steps a compressor with unloading performs (loaded, modulating, unloaded, idle).
-   **Cycle Time**: Amount of time for a compressor to complete one cycle.
-   **Dew Point**: Temperature at which moisture in air condenses at constant pressure.
-   **Discharge Pressure**: Pressure at the discharge connection of a compressor.
-   **Discharge Temperature**: Temperature at the discharge flange of the compressor.
-   **Filters**: Devices for separating and removing particulate matter, moisture, or entrained lubricant from air.
-   **Full Load**: Air compressor operation at full speed, delivering maximum air flow.
-   **Gauge Pressure**: Pressure determined by most instruments and gauges (psig).
-   **Intercooling**: Removal of heat from air or gas between compressor stages.
-   **Leak**: Unintended loss of compressed air to ambient conditions.
-   **Load/Unload Control**: Control method allowing compressor to run at full-load or no-load while driver maintains constant speed.
-   **Modulating Control**: System adapting to varying demand by throttling compressor inlet proportionally.
-   **Pneumatic Tools**: Tools operating by air pressure.
-   **Positive Displacement Compressors**: Compressors where air volume is mechanically reduced.
-   **Pressure**: Force per unit area (psi or kPa).
-   **Pressure Drop**: Loss of pressure in a compressed air system or component due to friction or restriction.
-   **Receiver**: Vessel or tank for storing gas under pressure.
-   **Reciprocating Compressor**: Compressor with a piston having reciprocating motion in a cylinder.
-   **Sequence**: Order in which compressors are brought online.
-   **Stages**: Series of steps in air or gas compression.
-   **Standard cfm**: Flow of free air measured and converted to standard reference conditions.
-   **Start/Stop Control**: System matching air supply to demand by starting and stopping the unit.
-   **Variable Speed Drive**: Electronic device to vary motor speed for energy savings at low flows.
-   **Unload/No load**: Compressor operation where no air is delivered due to closed or modified intake.

## Quick Tips to Optimize Compressed Air Systems

Here's a list of ten things to kick start your compressed air optimization and energy efficiency program:

1.  Identify and repair air leaks, starting with the biggest.
2.  Measure and baseline your compressed air system to determine operating costs and efficiency.
3.  Operate compressed air system at the lowest practical pressure.
4.  Install, adjust, and maintain automatic system controls to coordinate operations of air compressors.
5.  Turn off compressed air supply to zones, equipment, and applications that are not operating.
6.  Use a blower rather than a compressor where appropriate.
7.  Use air receivers to reduce compressor cycling and respond to peak air demands.
8.  Use larger diameter pipes and looped air distribution configurations where practical.
9.  Maintain compressed air equipment, filters, and drains at an appropriate level.
10. Constantly ask if compressed air is the best utility for the intended application.

**Remember to involve compressed air operating and maintenance personnel in production and end use decisions.**
