# Keeping The Heat In - Section 6: Basement insulation: floors, walls and crawl spaces

Basements can account for about **25 percent of a homes total heat loss**. This is due to the large, uninsulated surface area both above and below grade level. Contrary to popular opinion, earth is a poor insulator. There is also a lot of air leakage through basement windows and penetrations (including cracks in these areas) and at the top of the foundation wall (sill area and rim joist). *See Figure 6-1 for heat loss in a basement.*

Many basements have little or no insulation, so this means there is much potential for improvement. Insulating can often be tied in with other repairs or renovation work such as waterproofing, radon remediation or finishing the basement.

**Types of basement construction**

The most common type of construction is the full foundation basement, with mainly below grade foundation walls supporting the house structure (*see Figure 6-2 Common foundation types*). Many houses have been built with partial depth foundation walls that create a crawl space under the house. Some older homes, cottages or mobile homes are built up on posts and piers, leaving the space below the house open or walled off. Other houses are built on a slab-on-grade where there is no basement or crawl space at all. Consider obtaining expert advice before proceeding on remediation.

### Foundation Types:

*   **i) Concrete foundations**
    *   Poured or concrete block foundations (since 1920s), usually with parging, waterproofing, and drain tiles on the exterior.
    *   May need repair if older than 20 years.
    *   Can be insulated from outside or inside if no serious water or structural problems exist.
*   **ii) New houses or new foundations under older houses**
    *   Allow about a year of drying out time before insulating or renovating, as concrete contains large amounts of moisture.
*   **iii) Older rubble, brick or stone foundations**
    *   Often uneven, varying in depth and thickness.
    *   Rarely damp-proofed; high mortar content can absorb water.
    *   Best insulated from the outside, some interior insulating possible.
    *   *Always obtain expert advice before proceeding on remediation.*
*   **iv) Other types of foundations**
    *   Newer homes and additions may use preserved wood foundations, which are damp-proofed and generally fully insulated.

**Basement assessment**

Before planning the job, assess the status of your basement. Here are some of the problems to look for:

*   **i) Water leaks**
    *   **Major water leaks (persistent leaks, flooding)** must be corrected, often requiring excavation, damp-proofing/waterproofing, drainage system, and exterior insulation.
    *   **Minor water leaks** can sometimes be corrected by directing water away from the foundation (sloping grade, downspouts) and patching the interior foundation.
    *   Correct any problems with sump pumps or sewer backup before insulating.
*   **ii) Dampness**
    *   Symptoms: staining/mould, blistering/peeling paint, efflorescence (whitish mineral deposit), spalling (surface deterioration), musty smell.
    *   Minor dampness: may be corrected from the interior.
    *   More serious problems: should be corrected from the outside.
    *   Condensation: can form on foundation walls in summer with high humidity.

    *Checking for dampness:*
    *   Cut a plastic sheet (1 m / 40 in. square) and tape to concrete walls/slab. For earth floors, hold down edges with sand/sticks.
    *   After 1-2 days, check for condensation.
        *   **Condensation on top of plastic**: indicates high interior moisture levels.
        *   **Condensation under plastic**: indicates moisture movement from the ground into the home and possible radon emissions.
    *   Any subsequent renovation will require remediation (e.g., interior/exterior concrete sealing, subfloor membranes) or adding a moisture barrier.
*   **iii) Cracks**
    *   If an active crack (gets bigger/smaller), seek professional help for structural repairs.
*   **iv) Radon**
    *   May be present in all homes, with or without humidity/moisture problems.

**Insulate inside or outside?**

Insulating on the outside is best, but interior insulation is often necessary for economical and practical reasons. Sometimes a combination is required. Examine the advantages of each approach carefully.

*   **i) Insulating inside**
    *   May involve rigid insulation board and drywall, a wood-frame wall and insulation, or other combinations.
    *   Choice depends on moisture issues, need for moisture/air/vapour barriers, intended use of space, and cost.

    *Advantages of inside insulation:*
    *   Can be incorporated into basement finishing plans.
    *   Work can be done year-round, in stages, and often as DIY.
    *   Often easier and cheaper to insulate the full wall and achieve high insulating values.
    *   Landscaping and driveway will not be disturbed.

    *Disadvantages of inside insulation:*
    *   **Do not insulate a basement with moisture problems from the inside** (leaks, dampness, efflorescence, blistering paint) without correcting the moisture first.
    *   Interior insulation makes foundation walls colder, leading to condensation if humid air contacts them.
    *   Interior finishes can hide developing moisture problems, leading to long-term mould exposure.
    *   Obstructions (electrical panels, wiring, plumbing, stairs, partition walls) make work difficult and reduce insulation/air barrier effectiveness.

    *Technical note:*
    *   Concern about frost action and structural damage when insulating from the inside (frost penetrating deeper outside foundation wall) has been researched and found **not to be a problem** in most cases.
    *   *Check with local building authorities if your area has frost-susceptible soils or if neighbors have experienced difficulties.*

*   **ii) Insulating outside**
    *   Involves excavating around the foundation, waterproofing, and installing insulation (*as per Figure 6-3 Steps for exterior insulation*).
    *   Flashing must keep water from getting behind insulation; protective covering on exposed parts.

    *Advantages of outside insulation:*
    *   Outside wall is often more continuous and easier to insulate.
    *   Allows for effective identification and correction of moisture or structural problems (efflorescence, cracks, spalling, eroded mortar).
    *   Rubble or brick foundations and those with water/dampness issues **should be insulated from the outside**.
    *   Foundation repair, parging, waterproofing, and drainage system installation can be done simultaneously.
    *   No disruption inside the house, no interior work disturbed, no inside space lost.
    *   Freeze-thaw stresses are eliminated, frost unlikely to penetrate to footings.
    *   Foundation mass is within insulated portion, evening out temperature fluctuations.

    *Disadvantages of outside insulation:*
    *   Digging a trench by hand is difficult and risky; machinery may have access issues.
    *   Storing excavated dirt can be a problem.
    *   Excavation cannot be done in winter; may be problematic in spring or year-round with high water table.
    *   Features like non-removable steps, paved carports, shrubbery, trees, or fences can make work difficult.
    *   Rubble or brick foundations may be partially supported by soil; *get expert advice before starting*.
    *   Expensive to achieve high insulation levels; retrofit may detract from house appearance.

## 6.1 Insulating the basement from the outside

Before beginning work, assess:

*   Outside features (buried/surface services, access, lot lines).
*   Insulation requirements (type, thickness, height, depth).
*   Finishing details (protective coating, flashing).
*   Tools and equipment required.

### 6.1.1 Special safety considerations

*Refer to Part 1.4, [Health and safety considerations](/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/keeping-heat-section-1-introduction#a1-4), for general advice.*

*Safety warning:*
*   **Follow safe trenching practices** to avoid accidental burial and trench collapse. Protect trenches from water and elements; ensure people/animals cannot fall in.
*   Unstable soils may require bracing. *Consult local building authorities for advice on proper shoring and trenching.*
*   **Locate all underground services** (gas, electricity, telephone, water, sewage) before digging.

#### How to insulate outside the basement

The work may require several weeks. Plan extra time for excavation, crack repair, exterior waterproofing, and drainage system installation.

Steps:

*   **i) Dig the trench**
    *   Ideally to footings (never below).
    *   Wide enough to work in.
    *   Excavated dirt stored on tarp/polyethylene at least **600 mm (24 in.)** away.
    *   *Optional*: Install insulation to a minimum of **600 mm (24 in.)** below grade if foundation/drainage are good, for substantial thermal protection. Can be combined with interior insulation. An optional horizontal rigid insulation skirt can be added to reduce heat loss to the surface (*see Figure 6-4 Exterior basement insulation*).
*   **ii) Prepare the surface and site**
    *   Clean foundation surface (wire brush, scraper, pressure-washer).
    *   Inspect and repair major holes, cracks, damage; seal all penetrations.
    *   Smooth/replace deteriorated surfaces/parging; allow repairs to dry.
    *   Check/repair drainage tiles. Install new drainage system if needed (only if properly done to appropriate discharge); best done after foundation wall work. *Consult a drainage systems expert.*
    *   Have a contractor apply waterproofing material from grade level to footings, sealing all penetrations/overlaps (sheet materials, sprays, roll-on compounds). Follow manufacturer directions.
    *   Dimple membranes: can create drainage paths; some are not waterproof and need additional material. *Check with manufacturer/contractor for best product/installation.*
*   **iii) Apply the insulation**
    *   **Types used**: rigid mineral wool boards, high-density polystyrene (Type IV), polyurethane/polyisocyanurate boards.
    *   **Type IV polystyrene board** is most common for exterior below-grade applications (*see Figure 6-3 and Figure 6-4*).
    *   **Insulation with drainage capability (e.g., mineral wool board)** must only be used if:
        *   Applied to the full depth of the foundation wall.
        *   A drainage system is present.
        *   No horizontal seams break the drainage path.
    *   Measure and cut insulation to desired height (generally from top of footings to exterior wall flashing).
    *   Install starting at one corner, overlapping at corners, keeping sheets tight to wall (*see Figure 6-6 The insulation should overlap at corners*).
    *   *Technical note: Some experts suggest two layers of insulation with overlapping joints.*
    *   Insulation held by flashing at top edge, and corrosion-resistant fasteners/washers. Below-grade portion held by backfill (may need fasteners during process).
    *   Special interlocking systems (grooved polystyrene boards with steel channels) can be used above-grade only to **300 mm (12 in.)** depth. Special clips/fasteners available.
    *   For rubble/brick foundations, apply parging after repairs to smooth wall. Waterproof parging, insulate, then proceed.
*   **iv) Apply the flashing**
    *   Flashing holds insulation, prevents water penetration, provides clean junction.
    *   Consider location (how high insulation extends) and type of flashing (*see Figure 6-7 Types of flashing for a foundation  First floor transition*).
    *   **Standard Z-flashing**: if siding can be removed/pried up, insert at least **50 mm (2 in.)** behind siding/building paper.
    *   **Metal J-channel** (before insulation) or **wood flashing** (after insulation): if flashing cannot be inserted behind siding (e.g., brick).
    *   Flashing should accommodate insulation width and protective coat.
    *   Wood flashing: sloped with at least **20 mm ( in.)** overhang and drip edge.
    *   Seal joint between flashing and house with suitable caulking.
    *   Keep weep holes open for brick siding.
    *   Ideally, carry insulation up past header area by at least **150 mm (6 in.)** (*as shown in Figure 6-8 Insulating the header area from the exterior*). If not possible, air seal and insulate header area from interior.
*   **v) Protect the exterior by covering the exposed insulation**
    *   Cover insulation from top to at least **100 mm (4 in.)** below settled ground level (*as per Figure 6-9 Exterior protection should extend below grade*). **300 mm (12 in.)** below ground level may be preferable.
    *   **Covering options**:
        *   Expanded metal lath with cement parging.
        *   Polymer-modified pargings (directly on some insulation types).
        *   Pressure-treated plywood with stainless-steel fasteners.
        *   Vinyl or other siding matching the house.
*   **vi) Refill the excavation**
    *   Cover drain tiles with **150 mm (6 in.)** of clean gravel (**4 mm [1/8 in.]** or larger) and filter fabric. If drainage insulation is used, gravel should extend at least **100 mm (4 in.)** up insulation side.
    *   Backfill excavated area in stages, removing large objects, compacting/tamping ground.
    *   For poor draining soils (expansive clay), use free-draining backfill.
    *   Ground should slope away from house (e.g., **10 percent slope / 200 mm [8 in.] for first 2 m [6 ft.]**) to encourage drainage.
    *   Cover with patio stones, grass, or garden. Wait before expensive treatments due to settling.
*   **vii) Complete the finishing details**
    *   **Windows**: Wrap insulation around foundation to meet window frame. Apply lath and parging to edge of frame. Caulk joint between frame and parging. Sills should slope away from windows (*as shown in Figure 6-10 The sill should slope away from the window*).
    *   **Doors**: Outline with J-channel or equivalent flashing. May need to extend doorsill.
    *   **Penetrations**: Seal with compatible, flexible sealant to prevent wind, water, vermin entry.
*   **viii) Insulate the exterior joist header area**
    *   If exterior insulation does not extend above header area by at least **150 mm (6 in.)**, air seal and insulate header area from inside the basement (*see Part 6.2, [Insulating the basement from the inside](#a6-2)*).

### 6.1.2 Complications

*   **Cold cellar or unheated garage**: Apply insulation inside the basement, against cold cellar/garage walls, treating them like exterior basement walls. Weatherstrip and insulate doorway. Insulate ceiling of cold cellar/garage (*see Part 6.4, [Open foundations](#a6-4)*).
*   Extend outside insulation around rest of basement by at least **600 mm (24 in.)** beyond inside wall junction to minimize heat loss.
*   **Concrete porch, paved driveway, or other obstruction**: Switch insulation to the inside around obstacles. Allow at least **600 mm (24 in.)** overlap for continuous coverage and reduced heat loss through thermal bridge (*as illustrated in Figure 6-11 Overlapping foundation insulation at places where insulation cannot be put on the exterior*).

## 6.2 Insulating the basement from the inside

Consider the following factors:

*   Structural problems (cracks, bulges).
*   Insulation requirements (type, RSI (R) value, location).
*   Wiring and plumbing upgrades.
*   Finishing details.

*Consult local building authorities for code requirements and regional problems (e.g., frost heave due to expansive clay soils).*

**Common interior basement insulation types**: batt or blanket, glass fibre loose fill, polyurethane spray, rigid plastic board.

*   **Closed cell polyurethane spray foam**: high-quality insulating method for all wall types, including uneven ones. Can help control dampness. *Applied only by a certified installer.*
*   **Rigid plastic board insulation**: higher RSI value per millimetre than batt, requires less basement space and thinner supporting framework.

*Safety warning:*
*   *Refer to Part 1.4, [Health and safety considerations](/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/keeping-heat-section-1-introduction#a1-4), for guidelines.*
*   Powder-activated concrete fastening tools: *carefully follow usage directions.*

### 6.2.1 How to insulate inside the basement using only rigid board insulation

This method works best if the basement wall is even and vertical (e.g., poured concrete or concrete block).

#### Preparation

*   Air seal all leakage paths (sill, penetrations) after checking/repairing walls. This provides the primary air barrier.
*   Use **medium- to high-density foam board with good moisture resistance** (e.g., extruded polystyrene, Type IV expanded polystyrene) as insulation board acts as a moisture barrier.
*   *Check with manufacturer if foil-faced insulation board is compatible with cement/mortar.*

#### Installation

*   Seal insulation to foundation with foam-compatible adhesive around perimeter before fastening.
*   Air sealing foam board to wall creates an air and moisture barrier similar to spray foam.
*   Use wooden fastening strips on top of insulation to secure gypsum board. Alternatively, special wood/metal fastening strips that fit factory-cut grooves/notches in insulation panels.
*   Screw/nail drywall to strips secured to concrete with corrosion-resistant fasteners.
*   Install a minimum of **RSI 2.1 (R-12)** unless local codes require more.
*   Consider overlapping insulation layers to minimize heat loss through fastening strips.
*   Fit insulation snugly; use foam caulking and technical tape to seal all joints and interParts.

#### Finishing

*   Insulate and seal the joist area (unless joists are embedded in concrete). *See Part 6.2.6, [Joist header space](#a6-2-6).*
*   Protect entire wall with **12.7 mm (1/2-in.)** gypsum board or equivalent fire protection. Includes joist space if no new ceiling.

### 6.2.2 How to frame and insulate inside the basement

Three common methods for insulating with a frame wall:

*   Frame wall with single or double layer of batt insulation.
*   Frame wall filled with batt insulation and rigid board insulation backing.
*   Frame wall filled with batt insulation and spray foam backing.

#### Preparation

*   Air seal all leakage paths (sill, penetrations) after checking/repairing walls. This provides the primary air barrier.
*   *For information on materials and techniques, see Section 3, Materials, and Section 4, Comprehensive air leakage control.*

*Technical note:*
*   Use dry lumber for framing. If not dry, allow framing to dry for at least two weeks before adding insulation/covering wall. Temporary bracing may be needed to prevent twisting.

### 6.2.3 Frame wall with single or double layer of batt insulation

*   Cover basement walls with **house wrap** to protect insulation, framing, and wall finish from water damage. (Plastic was once used but can cause condensation/mould).

*Safety warning:*
*   **Do not use asphalt building paper (tar paper) inside the house** due to toxic vapours.

*   House wrap allows moisture to dry out towards the interior or to the top of foundations above grade.
*   Building paper should start at/just above grade line, extend to basement floor and under bottom plate of frame wall. Mechanically support with strapping (e.g., 1 x 3 lumber) (*see Figure 6-13 Framed wall with batt insulation*).
*   House wrap must be installed correctly for inward breathing/diffusion. *Consult manufacturers guide for proper installation.*

#### Framing

Two approaches:

*   **1. Install wall close to but not touching house wrap** (**12-mm [-in.]** spacing) using 2 x 4 or 2 x 6 lumber.
*   **2. Position framing further away** to allow a layer of batt insulation between framing and wall.
    *   This takes more interior room but provides more insulation, less thermal bridging, and better moisture protection. The bottom plate should sit on top of house wrap extension and on a continuous impermeable membrane (e.g., sill gasket) (*see Figure 6-14 Double layer batt insulation in a framed wall*).
*   Fasten top plate to bottom of joists. For walls parallel to joists, build nailing support for top plate (*Figure 6-15 Top plate detail where the joists run parallel to the wall shows one approach*).
*   Square up walls.
*   For expansive clay soils: may need **25 mm (1 in.)** gap between top plate and floor joists for vertical movement. *Consult local building authorities.*
*   Install studs **600 mm (24 in.)** on centre. Ensure structural support for finishing. Studs must be vertical and accurately spaced.
*   Extra framing needed around doors; window openings need single stud as wall is non-load bearing.
*   If perfectly level/square with no obstructions: build wall on floor, tilt into place, shim bottom plate, secure frame.
*   Install wiring and plumbing rough-ins.

#### Insulating

*   If space left behind frame for batt insulation: add insulation between studs and wall in a horizontal layer, tight against foundation wall.
*   Fill frame wall with vertical layer of batt insulation, snugly between studs. Install insulation at back of wall with no gaps, air spaces, or over-compression.
*   Alternatively: use fibreglass blown-in insulation, filling cavities to manufacturers recommended density and RSI level.

#### Finishing

*   Install a **vapour barrier** over studs and insulation.
    *   **Polyethylene vapour barrier**: suitable for proven dry basements.
    *   **Alternate 1: Polyamide sheeting (Nylon-6 vapour retarder film or smart barrier)**: breathable membrane with variable water vapour permeance. Allows wall to dry toward interior if humidity increases. *Follow manufacturers directions and installation requirements.*
        *   Leave enough film at top to connect to air barrier in joist header space.
        *   Seal all edges, seams, and penetrations with acoustical sealant or approved materials. Overlap joints over a stud, seal with continuous bead of sealant, staple through bead (*see Figure 4-3 detail*).
    *   **Alternate 2: Airtight drywall approach (ADA)**: uses rigid materials (typically drywall) carefully sealed to framing and connections with adhesive-backed foam tape and flexible caulking.
        *   Attention to detail is critical. Works only if completely sealed and tied into house's air/vapour barrier system.
        *   **ADA sealing considerations (non-inclusive list)**:
            *   Frame airtight boxes for plumbing, water, drain line penetrations; caulk pipe penetrations (*see Figure 3-6 and Figure 6-16 Sealed plumbing penetrations*). If no airtight box, seal penetrations to backing and caulk between backing and wall finish.
            *   Use special airtight electrical switch/plug boxes with gaskets; caulk wire penetrations.
            *   Do not run wiring/plumbing from exterior to interior walls unless all holes are caulked.
            *   Air seal window/door frames with expanding foam and caulking (*see Figure 7-7*).
            *   Install foam gaskets and caulk upper edge of top plate and all other framing members in contact with floors, slab, interior walls, ceiling.
            *   Air seal edges and perforations of obstructions (e.g., stairs) against exterior wall.
            *   Frame in and air seal separately any non-surface mounted electrical panels; air seal all penetrations.
            *   *Remember to insulate and seal the joist header space (Part 6.2.6, [Joist header space](#a6-2-6)) before finishing surface. This area is prone to air leakage.*
            *   Finishing must include a vapour barrier primer or paint.

### 6.2.4 Frame wall with batt insulation and rigid board insulation

This method involves gluing rigid board insulation to the foundation wall, then covering with a frame wall incorporating batt insulation. Results in higher insulation values, less interior space loss, reduced thermal bridging, and no need for additional moisture barrier.

*   Works best if basement wall is even and vertical (e.g., poured concrete or concrete block).
*   Use only moisture-resistant board insulation (e.g., extruded polystyrene or Type IV expanded polystyrene).

#### Preparation

*   Follow preparations from Part 6.2.1.

#### Installation

*   Using rigid board insulation with at least **RSI 1.76 (R-10)**, secure and seal to foundation with foam-compatible adhesive around perimeter.
*   Air sealing foam board to wall creates an air and moisture barrier similar to spray foam. Special mechanical fasteners can be used.
*   Install insulation snugly; use urethane foam sealant and technical tape to seal all joints and interParts.
*   Install wood-frame wall directly in contact with rigid board insulation. Follow techniques in Part 6.2.3.

#### Adding additional insulation

*   Frame wall can be roughed in for wiring/plumbing and insulated as in Part 6.2.3.
*   For joist area, see Part 6.2.6, [Joist header space](#a6-2-6).

#### Finishing

*   **Do not use a polyethylene air and vapour barrier** (risk of double vapour barrier with foam board).
*   Instead, use either **smart barrier** or the **ADA method**.
*   If using smart barrier, leave enough film at top to connect to air barrier in joist space.
*   If using ADA method, pay strict attention to proper air and vapour sealing, including a layer of vapour barrier paint.
*   Any exposed foam insulation may require fire protection per code.

### 6.2.5 Frame wall with batt insulation and spray foam backing

Popular hybrid method. Involves building a frame wall **25 to 50 mm (1 to 2 in.)** away from foundation, then a professional sprays about **50 mm (2 in.)** of medium/high density polyurethane foam against the wall.

*   Spray foam fills/bonds to framing/wall, offering high insulation value (**about RSI 0.98 [R-5.6/in.]**), reducing thermal bridging, and providing a moisture barrier.
*   Frame wall is then filled with insulation.
*   Primary limitation: higher cost, though reduces need for additional materials/labour (*see Figure 6-17 Top view of a framed wall with batt insulation and spray foam*).

#### Preparation

*   Discuss with spray foam contractor what to do before spraying.
*   Address major issues (active structural cracks, frequent/major water leaks) first.
*   Install wood frame wall at distance from foundation as directed by contractor, including corner studs. Follow framing techniques in Part 6.2.3.

#### Adding additional insulation

*   After foam installation, frame wall can be roughed in for wiring/plumbing and insulated. Polyethylene vapour barrier not recommended.
*   For joist area, see Part 6.2.6, [Joist header space](#a6-2-6).
*   For finishing, see previous frame wall method in Part 6.2.3.

### 6.2.6 Joist header space

Also called rim joist space, foundation header space, or joist space. This is where floor joists intersect and are supported by foundation walls in basements/crawl spaces  where house structure rests on foundation.

*   Prone to air leakage, seldom properly insulated, causing drafts, dust/pollen, vermin access.
*   Concrete block foundations: prone to leakage through center holes. Ensure tops of concrete block foundations and other penetrations are sealed.

**Three major joint configurations and implications for air sealing/insulation**:

*   Floor joists on a sill plate (*Figure 6-18, image A and Figure 6-19, image A*)
*   Floor joists partially embedded in the foundation (*Figure 6-18, image B and Figure 6-19, image B*)
*   Floor joists fully embedded in the foundation (*Figure 6-18, image C and Figure 6-19, image C*)

*   If exterior foundation insulation doesn't cover full header joist area, space must be insulated and sealed from inside.
*   If foundation walls are insulated from interior, air/vapour barrier must be continuous for wall and header space.
*   *Figure 6-19 Insulating the joist header area shows how to air seal and insulate three common types of joist header spaces.*
*   For fully-embedded joists, **do not exceed a maximum of 25 mm (1 in.) of foam board insulation** to prevent floor from becoming uncomfortably cold and prone to damage.
*   Building codes may mandate insulation levels; *check with local authorities*.
*   **Polyurethane spray foam** by certified contractor offers excellent air sealing and insulation. Must be covered with fire-resistant material if not covered by basement ceiling.

### 6.2.7 Complications

Issues that can complicate insulation installation:

*   **Wall space interrupted by pipes, ducts or an electrical panel**
    *   Move water lines away from wall or install insulation/vapour barrier behind pipes (on warm side). **Never place insulation in front of pipes**.
    *   Pipes through air/vapour barrier: pass through plywood box sealed to main barrier, gaps caulked.
    *   **Do not insulate around flue pipes**. Check manufacturer/specialist for clearances.
    *   **Do not insulate if proper clearances cannot be maintained** between furnaces, wood stoves, and wall.
    *   **Electrical panels**: Be careful; panel is live even if house power is off. Have electrician help seal/move panel.
*   **Basement wall interrupted by a window**
    *   Seal point where window frame adjoins wall with low-expansion spray foam caulk. Seal frames to vapour barrier system.

### 6.2.8 Rubble and irregular basement and crawl space walls

Irregular basements (stone/rubble) are rarely waterproofed on outside.

*   Exterior retrofit always recommended. Interior insulation possible if no water/moisture problems.
*   First, cover interior wall with cement-based parging to smooth surface and protect mortar.
*   Build frame wall, add a maximum of **RSI 2.1 (R-12)** batt insulation, and finish as described in Part 6.2.3.
*   **Do not go with higher insulation values** due to risk of damaging freeze-thaw cycles. *Check with local building authorities.*
*   **Closed-cell spray polyurethane foam**: successfully used to reduce dampness for rubble walls, offering thermal protection. **Do not exceed RSI 2.1 (R-12)**. *Installed by certified installer and covered by fire-resistant material.*
*   For very wet basements prone to flooding/high moisture: may be best not to insulate walls. *Consult building authorities/contractor about treating floor joist space as exposed/insulated floor.*
*   Additional modifications needed for plumbing and heating system.

### 6.2.9 Part of the basement is a cold cellar or an unheated garage

*   Apply insulation to cold cellar/garage wall separating heated from unheated space, treating it as an external wall.
*   Weatherstrip and insulate doorway from basement.
*   Insulate ceiling as described in Part 6.4, [Open foundations](#a6-4), and overlap insulation as in Figure 6-11.
*   If insulating a cold cellar, check winter temperature to prevent freezing. If room too warm or floor above cold, consider insulating cold room ceiling.

### 6.2.10 Basement pony walls

A pony wall is a short wood frame wall on top of a conventional concrete foundation.

*   Wood frame part: insulated between studs.
*   Concrete part: insulated on interior (assuming no moisture problems).
*   Insulation on concrete extends up about **200 mm (8 in.)** to overlap frame part, creating a ledge (*see Figure 6-20 Insulating a pony wall is a two-step process that creates a small ledge*).
*   To avoid a ledge: install a frame wall from floor to ceiling and fill cavity with insulation. This offers higher thermal performance but creates a deep window frame.

### 6.2.11 Overhangs

*   Seal and insulate floors that overhang the foundation.
*   Remove finish underneath overhang, air seal space between joists above foundation with polyurethane foam or caulked, low-permeability rigid insulation.
*   Insulate joist space with batt or blanket insulation, add air barrier before re-installing finish (*as shown in Figure 6-21 Overhangs should be air sealed before they are insulated*).
*   Alternatively: apply layer of rigid board insulation with protective outer finish to underside of overhang to reduce thermal bridging (joist space should still be air sealed/insulated).
*   Contractor can spray foam into cavities or blow in dense-pack fibre insulation through drilled holes.
*   If cavity can be made air tight, a warmer floor above an overhang can be created by leaving a small air gap between insulation top and flooring bottom. This allows convection currents to warm the floor. (Note: this decreases insulation amount, leading to additional heat loss; offset by adding rigid insulation to underside of overhang).

### 6.2.12 Basement floors

*   Most heat lost through upper part of foundation walls; basement floor slabs seldom insulated.
*   For improved comfort, moisture control, and radon reduction: apply a moisture barrier to floor or seal floor to prevent moisture accumulation between insulation and slab.
*   Alternatively: install a floor drainage system under new floor.
*   If installing/replacing concrete slab: excellent opportunity for in-floor heating or making it radiant-ready (insulation and heating lines installed before pouring new slab). *Consult heating contractor experienced in hydronic in-floor heating.*

### 6.2.13 Uninhabited basements and crawl spaces

*   Medium- and high-density polyurethane foams used in basements/crawl spaces with poured concrete, concrete block, brick, or rubble walls not intended as living space.
*   Spray foam installed directly on foundation wall, then sprayed with non-flammable covering to meet local code.

## 6.3 Crawl spaces

Two ways to insulate a crawl space:

*   Walls of crawl space insulated (inside or outside), resulting in a heated area.
*   House floor above it insulated to keep heat from getting into crawl space.

*   Treat heated crawl spaces as short basements; renovate as described earlier.
*   Fix any water leaks and remove sources of water infiltration (*see Part 2.4, [Control of moisture flow](/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/keeping-heat-section-2-your-house-works#a2-4)*).
*   **Never vent a clothes dryer into a crawl space**.
*   Insulating walls is recommended to avoid insulating/protecting plumbing/heating systems.
*   Walls can be insulated externally to reduce internal moisture problems and keep soil below footings warm. Easier than insulating ceiling, especially in tight spaces. Requires fewer materials than ceilings.
*   If no moisture barrier on crawl space floor, add one. Minimum: **0.10 mm (4 mil)** clear or opaque polyethylene, overlapped, caulked, and taped at seams.
*   Mechanically fasten moisture barrier to walls and obstructions. Seal to any wall moisture barrier. Hold down with old boards or smooth scrap material to prevent billowing. Protect with plastic floor mat if traffic expected. **Do not use sand or gravel**.

### 6.3.1 How to insulate a heated crawl space

#### From the outside

*   Insulate outside wall exactly as for outside basement wall (*see Figure 6-22 and Part 6.1, [Insulating the basement from the outside](#a6-1)*).
*   If outside obstructions prevent encircling crawl space, insulate inside portion of wall at those points. Overlap interior and exterior insulation coverage by at least **600 mm (24 in.)** (*see Figure 6-11*).
*   If crawl space does not open into full basement, it should have ventilation. **Do not use exterior vents** (risk of condensation). Incorporate with whole-house ventilation or use a dehumidifier. Existing vents should be sealed permanently if space will be heated.
*   If foundation footings are above frost line, insulate outside of crawl space walls. Exterior insulation keeps walls warmer, avoiding frost heave. Shallow footings can be kept warmer with horizontal insulation sloping away from foundation.

#### From the inside

*   If using rigid board or spray foam, follow approach outlined for inside of a basement (*see Part 6.2, [Insulating the basement from the inside](#a6-2)*).
*   Treat joist spaces as described in Part 6.2.2, [How to frame and insulate inside the basement](#a6-2-2).
*   If foam insulation is used, *ask building inspector if fire protective coverings are required*.
*   Apply polyethylene moisture barrier to crawl space floor. Ensure space is adequately ventilated as noted above.

### 6.3.2 How to insulate a partially heated crawl space

Insulating between and under joists to create an unheated crawl space can lead to problems (freezing pipes, frozen ground, rot). Use floor insulation only when combined with foundation-wall insulation to create a partially heated crawl space (*as per Figure 6-23 Insulation on the walls and in the floor creates a partially heated crawl space*).

*   Insulation board under floor joists reduces thermal bridging and increases floor insulation level.
*   Similar to Section 6.2.11, [Overhangs](#a6-2-11): if cavity can be made air tight, warmer floor by leaving air space between insulation top and flooring bottom.
*   This method decreases insulation value in joist space, but offset by adding rigid insulation underneath.
*   *Check local building code if protective covering required for exposed insulation board.*

#### Points of general importance

*   Air and vapour barrier must be on the **warm (top) side of insulation**.
*   If floor above crawl space has impermeable material (vinyl, linoleum, plywood), it already serves as air/vapour barrier. Locate and seal air leaks (piping, wiring holes).
*   Air tightness at rim joist area is critical; seal with polyurethane foam.
*   Batt insulation may be held in place with breathable building wrap, chicken wire, polystyrene bead board (Type I or II), or commercial insulation-support system.
*   Place insulation tightly against underside of floor, filling joist cavity to support system.
*   Tape seams in heating ducts; insulate all ducts and water pipes. Even insulated pipes may freeze if crawl space temperature drops below freezing. Consider energy-efficient, self-regulating electric heat trace for plumbing pipes.
*   Ensure crawl space is adequately ventilated in spring (ratio of **1 to 500** vent area to floor area). **Do not ventilate in winter**; plug and insulate vents. Inspect/monitor humidity/condition monthly.
*   Moisture barrier required on crawl space floor.
*   For full basements with a crawl space part where part of floor above is insulated: insulate wall separating basement from crawl space.
*   If ground level inside crawl space is lower than outside: risk of frost heave damaging walls. Keep water away from foundation walls.
*   As safety precaution: install thermostat attached to small heater in crawl space to prevent freezing.

## 6.4 Open foundations

Some older homes, cottages, and modular/mobile homes have open foundations and exposed floors.

*   Insulate between joists same way as crawl spaces, with vapour barrier above insulation (on warm side).
*   Good air seal required; insulation protected from wind, insects, animals.
*   Possible to build insulated skirt around foundation to create heated crawl space.
*   Modular/mobile homes: floor may contain bagged insulation system. Critical to seal perforations/openings, especially around water service line, sewer connection, gas/oil service, and furnace combustion air inlet.
*   If joist space already covered: consider blowing dense-pack fibre insulation so no air space between insulation and floor above. Alternatively, medium- or high-density foam can be used as vapour barrier, air barrier, and primary insulation layer.
*   **Do not vent clothes dryer into floor space**.

## 6.5 Concrete slab on grade

Insulation applied to houses with slab-on-grade foundations exactly as for outside of a full basement (*see Part 6.1, [Insulating the basement from the outside](#a6-1)*).

*   If foundation is on frost-susceptible soils: install a layer of impermeable insulation horizontally in the soil at a slope ratio of **1:5 for at least 1 m (3 ft.)** (*as per Figure 6-24 Sloped rigid insulation buried in the soil provides frost protection to the slab-on-grade*).
*   *Consult local building authority for more specific information.*
*   A dry concrete slab helps limit mould, water damage, and musty odours. Perform dampness test (described at beginning of chapter) even if slab appears dry.
