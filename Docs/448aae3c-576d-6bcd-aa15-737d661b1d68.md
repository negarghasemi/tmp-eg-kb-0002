# Keeping The Heat In - Section 7: Insulating Walls

Walls are a significant factor in a property's energy efficiency, accounting for approximately **20 percent of heat loss** in houses. Beyond heat conduction, uncontrolled air leakage through cracks and penetrations in walls also contributes to energy inefficiency. This section provides guidance on insulating walls during renovations or new construction to improve a home's energy performance.

## Types of Wall Construction

Canadian houses primarily use three types of wall construction, each with different insulation considerations:

### i) Solid Walls

*   **Construction**: Built of brick, stone, concrete block, log, or wood plank.
*   **Insulation Challenge**: These walls typically lack cavities that can be insulated internally.
*   **Options**: Insulation must be added to either the **exterior** or **interior** surfaces.
*   **Critical Warning**: Some solid walls (e.g., double brick, wood plank) have small cavities (generally less than 25 mm / 1 in.) that serve as drainage planes. **Never insulate these cavities or plug their drain holes**, as this can lead to moisture problems.

### ii) Concrete Block Walls

*   **Construction**: Usually feature hollow cores.
*   **Energy Issue**: Hollow cores allow air circulation, which increases convective heat losses. The block's internal webs and mortar act as thermal bridges, limiting the effectiveness of insulating the cores alone.
*   **Recommendation**: **Seal all possible air leakage routes** into the block wall and ensure the top course of blocks is properly capped. For party walls, ensure the space between the block wall and interior finish is fully air-sealed.

### iii) Frame Walls

*   **Construction**: Have cavities that are suitable for insulation.
*   **Obstacles**: Internal elements like top/bottom plates, fire stops, blocking, plumbing, wiring, and heating ducts can interfere with insulation installation.
*   **Brick Veneer**: If a frame house has a brick veneer, there is typically a 25 to 50 mm (1 to 2 in.) air space between the bricks and the frame wall for drainage. **Do not insulate this space**.
*   **Exterior Insulation**: For frame walls, it is generally more effective to add insulation directly to the exterior of the frame wall, incorporating an appropriate air barrier, drainage plane, and siding.

## Opportunities for Upgrading Wall Insulation

*   **Dense-Pack Techniques**: Empty or partially insulated frame wall cavities can be insulated using dense-pack techniques (e.g., cellulose, glass fibre, approved foams) from the top, bottom, interior, or exterior.
*   **Air Sealing**: Insulating efforts should always include comprehensive air sealing.
*   **Renovation Integration**: Wall insulation is often best integrated into larger renovation projects, such as interior wall repairs, electrical upgrades, or exterior re-siding.
*   **Pre-Insulation Checks**: Before insulating, **address any existing moisture or structural problems** (e.g., staining, mould, rot, cracks, improperly operating windows/doors).
*   **Vapour Barriers**: Pay close attention to vapour barriers. They must be located on the **warm one-third of the finished wall** (adhering to the 1/3  2/3 rule). Assess the location and condition of existing vapour barriers (e.g., old plaster walls with paint).

## 7.1 Blown-in Insulation

Professional insulation contractors can significantly improve wall insulation using blown-in loose-fill or injected foam insulation.

### Key Aspects

*   **Materials**: Cellulose, glass fibre, and approved foams are used.
*   **Technique**: **Dense-pack methods** are used to compress existing batt insulation (if present), fill remaining voids, and provide good air sealing.
*   **Effectiveness**: While thermal resistance gains might be limited in already partially insulated walls (due to typical stud space thickness of 89 to 102 mm / 3  to 4 in.), good results can be achieved, especially when combined with interior or exterior insulation upgrades.
*   **Pre-installation Check**: To determine existing insulation, consider looking behind electrical outlets (with power off).

### Installation Process

1.  **Drilling Holes**: Small holes (15 to 50 mm /  to 2 in.) are drilled into each stud space. Typically, one to five holes per storey are needed, including above and below windows and doors.
2.  **Injection**: A long tube is inserted into the cavity from the top or bottom and withdrawn in stages to ensure proper density filling.

### Product Selection & Contractor Engagement

*   Review the characteristics of blown-in and foamed-in insulations (refer to Section 3, Materials).
*   Choose a contractor with a proven track record.
*   **Contract Requirement**: Stipulate the manufacturers recommended densities in the contract. Agree on the number of bags/containers to be used.
*   **Quality Control**: Small variations are acceptable, but too little insulation can lead to voids/settling, and too much can cause walls to bulge. Ensure proper hole sealing, patching, and finishing.

### Installation Locations

*   **i) From the Inside**:
    *   Small holes (15 to 50 mm /  to 2 in.) are drilled through the interior wall finish.
    *   Insulation is blown or injected directly into the wall.
    *   Holes must be completely plugged and sealed.
    *   If replacing the interior finish, you can also add a well-sealed air and vapour barrier before applying new drywall.
*   **ii) From the Outside**:
    *   Exterior siding can be drilled, lifted, or removed for access.
    *   For brick siding, single bricks can be temporarily removed.
    *   **Crucially, do not insulate the drainage cavity** between brick veneer and stud walls.
    *   Ensure the installer patches holes section by section to prevent water entry.
*   **iii) From the Basement/Attic**:
    *   This is often the easiest method, especially for balloon frame construction with open cavities from top to bottom.
    *   All stud spaces need filling, accounting for obstructions like windows, doors, fire stops, and cross braces.
    *   The contractor determines optimal filling direction (top, bottom, or both).
    *   Access holes must be tightly sealed after filling.

## 7.2 Renovating the Interior

When undertaking extensive interior renovations, there are two primary approaches to insulating walls.

### 7.2.1 Rebuild the Existing Wall

This method involves opening up the existing wall structure.

1.  **Preparation**: Remove existing wallboard or plaster from a wood frame wall.
2.  **Cavity Insulation**: Add or upgrade insulation within the existing wall cavity.
3.  **Vapour Barrier & Finish**: Install a vapour barrier, new drywall, and finish.
4.  **Enhanced Insulation**: To reduce thermal bridging and increase insulation, add horizontal strapping (e.g., 50 mm x 50 mm, 50 mm x 76 mm, or 50 mm x 89 mm) and fill the new space with insulation.
5.  **Vapour Barrier Placement**: The vapour barrier must be on the **warm side of the wall**, within the first third of the overall wall insulation's thermal resistance value (the 1/3  2/3 rule).
6.  **Rigid Foam Board Alternative**: After insulating the cavity, rigid foam board can be screwed or nailed directly to exposed studs. These boards offer high RSI values and act as a thermal break. Depending on the type and thickness, the foam board itself may act as a vapour barrier, or a separate approved vapour barrier may be needed.
7.  **Professional Spray Foam**: If using professional spray foam:
    *   **Low-density spray foam**: Remove existing cavity insulation (or strap the wall for a deeper cavity) and fill. Low-density foam acts as an air barrier, but **requires an additional vapour barrier** before finishing.
    *   **High-density spray foam**: Strap the wall to create a deeper cavity. Spray a continuous layer (minimum 50 mm / 2 in.) against the exterior sheathing and framing. Fill the remaining cavity with other insulation (e.g., low-density spray foam, glass, mineral, or cellulose fibre). **High-density spray foam acts as a vapour barrier**, so no additional polyethylene is needed. Install a smart barrier or use the ADA method before finishing.

### 7.2.2 Build a New Wall on the Inside of an Existing One

This method is suitable for both wood frame and masonry walls, creating an entirely new insulated wall parallel to the existing one.

1.  **Construction**: Build a new frame wall at any desired distance from the old wall, ensuring all cavities are filled with insulation.
2.  **Vapour Barrier**: If a new, well-sealed vapour barrier is installed, there's no need to remove or damage any existing vapour barrier in the old wall.
3.  **Process**: Follow instructions for insulating a basement from the inside (refer to Part 6.2). This includes:
    *   Sealing air leaks.
    *   Framing the new wall.
    *   Framing around window and door openings.
    *   Insulating, ideally in two layers to avoid gaps.
    *   Installing an air and vapour barrier.
    *   Applying the new finish.

### Additional Considerations for Interior Renovations

*   **Air and Vapour Barrier Coverage**: Cover the entire wall area, including window and door openings. Cut an 'X' from corner to corner at openings and seal the flaps to the frame. Ensure all joints, openings, and penetrations are well-sealed.
*   **Obstructions**: Plan to extend insulation behind pipes, electrical boxes, and heating ducts to place them on the warm side of the insulation. Move electrical boxes to the new wall and install them in poly pans sealed to the new air and vapour barrier. Plumbing pipes should be moved or carefully insulated from behind to prevent freezing. Air duct joints must be sealed with approved sealants.
*   **Sealing Gaps**: Seal cracks around door or window frames with non-expanding polyurethane foam sealant, or use insulation/backer rod and caulk.
*   **Partition Walls**: Consider extending insulation past junctions at partition walls. This involves removing drywall, cutting back the partition, extending insulation, and sealing the air and vapour barrier through the partition.
*   **Fast Option for Increased RSI**: For a quick upgrade, install at least RSI 0.88 to 1.76 (R-5 to 10) rigid board insulation directly to the wall finish and cover with drywall. Ensure the vapour barrier is sealed and reposition electrical components. Simultaneously, consider blown or injected insulation into existing cavities.

## 7.3 Renovating the Exterior

Exterior renovation presents an excellent opportunity to add substantial insulation.

### Best Practices

*   **Comprehensive Upgrade**: Ideally, remove existing siding to perform structural, wiring, plumbing, cavity insulation, and vapour/air barrier upgrades before installing additional insulation and new siding.
*   **Overlay Option**: Alternatively, rigid board or batt/blanket insulation can be applied directly on top of old siding.

### Key Considerations

*   **Insulation Levels**: Significant amounts of insulation can be added, as space is usually less restricted than indoors (except for lot-line restrictions or limited roof overhangs).
*   **Extensions**: Account for extending window and door jambs, and other penetrations (vents, electrical service, pipes, eavestroughs, downpipes).
*   **Rim Joist Area**: Extend the new insulated wall past the rim joist area to seal this common air leakage point. Extend at least 200 mm (8 in.) above grade, or 600 mm (24 in.) below ground level over the foundation wall if the basement is uninsulated (refer to Part 6.1).
*   **Cost-Effectiveness**: The material cost of exterior insulation is typically only 15% to 20% of the total retrofit cost. Given this, consider installing higher levels of insulation (e.g., R-15 to R-20 instead of R-4 to R-10).
*   **Vapour Barrier Placement**: If the new insulation's R-value is at least double that of the old wall, a new vapour barrier (continuous polyethylene sheet or high-density spray foam) can be installed over the old wall before new construction.
*   **Air Barrier Placement**: A new air barrier (e.g., spun polyolefin) can be installed on the exterior, leaving the existing vapour barrier inside. Ensure all penetrations, seams, joints, and perimeter edges are sealed. If extending below the rim joist, seal and mechanically fix it to the foundation.
*   **Air Space**: Limit any air space between the existing wall and new construction to no more than 10 mm (3/8 in.) to allow moisture escape. Be aware this space can reduce insulation effectiveness by about 5 percent.
*   **New Exterior Framing**: Requires a top plate along the soffit and a bottom plate or box beam attached to the foundation wall. Ensure the top air barrier is sealed to the old wall and maintain adequate roof ventilation if soffit ventilation is altered.
*   **Eaves**: Ensure eaves prevent wind-blown water from entering between insulation and siding. Add flashing and caulk top joints if necessary.

### Rigid Board Insulation

*   **Material Selection**: Various types can be used, but check local building codes for minimum RSI values and air/vapour barrier requirements. Impermeable foam board with a true RSI value greater than 1.14/25 mm (R-6.5/in.) may be suspect (refer to Part 3.1).
*   **Installation**: Fasten securely as per manufacturer specifications, ensuring a snug fit without gaps.
*   **Siding Fasteners**: Check siding manufacturer recommendations for required fastener type and length over rigid board insulation.
*   **Insulated Siding**: An alternative, but typically offers low insulating values (RSI 0.35 to 0.70 / R-2 to 4). It should include a drainage feature; if not, add a drainage plane.
*   **Strapping**: Applying vertical or diagonal strapping on top of the insulation aids drainage, reduces heat buildup behind siding, provides a nailing surface, and reduces thermal bridging.

### Batt/Blanket Insulation

*   **Wooden Framework**: Build a wooden framework on top of the entire outside wall to hold the insulation and support the new siding. Ensure proper air and vapour barrier installation.
*   **Larsen Truss Alternative**: A lightweight frame wall (e.g., Larsen truss) can be hung from rafters or supported by a bottom plate. This allows for two layers of snugly installed batt insulation (one horizontal, one vertical), achieving RSI 3.5 (R-20) or higher.

## 7.4 Miscellaneous Spaces: Attached Garages and More

Insulation and air sealing are crucial where heated spaces adjoin unheated areas.

### Attached Unheated Garage

*   **Walls, Ceiling, Door**: The walls, ceiling, and door shared with the house must be insulated and air-sealed to minimize heat loss and prevent garage fumes from entering the living space.
    *   **Above-ground walls**: Refer to Part 7.1 (Blown-in insulation) and Part 7.2 (Renovating the interior).
    *   **Below-ground walls**: Refer to Section 6 (Basement insulation).
*   **Open Garage Ceiling**: If joists are exposed, follow guidelines for open foundations (refer to Part 6.4). It's best to remove any existing finish for proper air sealing and insulation.
*   **Finished Garage Ceiling**: Dense-pack insulation can be blown into the space between the ceiling and the floor above. All holes cut in the ceiling must be carefully re-sealed to prevent fumes from entering rooms above. All house ducts must be sealed with approved sealant.
*   **Safety Warning**: **Never run heating supply and return ducts from the house into a garage**, as this is dangerous and violates building codes (refer to Section 9).
*   **Professional Spray Foam (Exposed Joists)**: Recommended against the bottom of the floor above for effective air and vapour sealing and initial insulation. Fill any remaining space with additional insulation. Exposed spray foam must be covered with drywall or approved fire-rated overspray.
*   **Existing Finished Ceiling (Not Removing)**: If the surface is even, rigid board insulation and drywall can be nailed to it. Crucially, **seal all potential leakage paths** (especially at ceiling/wall junctions) with spray foam or impermeable rigid board insulation caulked between joists before adding rigid board insulation.

## 7.5 Additions and New Construction

New construction offers an ideal opportunity to integrate continuous air and vapour barriers and high levels of insulation efficiently and cost-effectively.

### Core Principles

*   **Continuity**: Both insulation and air barriers should run continuously without breaks or thermal bridging.
*   **Future Planning**: Consider current and future renewable energy systems (solar water heating, passive solar, in-floor heating, photovoltaic power, EV charging stations) early in the design process.
    *   Ensure roof structures can support additional weight or wind loads.
    *   Plan for future conduits, water piping, or electrical circuits.

### Specific Components

*   **Attic**:
    *   Features: High insulation levels, continuous air and vapour barrier, and proper ventilation.
    *   Trusses: Use raised heel trusses (for flat ceilings), scissor trusses (for cathedral ceilings), or parallel chord trusses (for flat roofs) to allow for high insulation levels above the outer walls' top plates.
*   **Walls**:
    *   Construction: Common systems include 2x6 walls with insulating sheathing, interior cross strapping, double-wall systems, and trusses.
    *   Barriers: These systems allow for the continuous air and vapour barrier to be integrated partway into the wall.
    *   Headers: Recessed headers provide space for continuous air and vapour barriers and extra insulation.
*   **Windows**:
    *   Type: Utilize high-performance **ENERGY STAR** windows.
    *   Sealing: All windows must be sealed to the air barrier.
*   **Foundation**:
    *   Insulation: Implement full-depth insulation (e.g., on the exterior).
    *   Moisture Control: Ensure proper damp proofing, a drainage system, and a sloped grade for a dry basement.
*   **Mechanical Systems**:
    *   Sizing: Space heating and cooling systems can be smaller in well-insulated and air-sealed homes.
    *   Efficiency: High-efficiency space and water heating equipment can use directly connected combustion air, avoiding the use of household air.
