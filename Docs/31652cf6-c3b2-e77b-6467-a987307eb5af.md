# Keeping The Heat In - Section 4: Comprehensive air leakage control in your home

Air leakage control is the single most important retrofit activity and should be considered first in any upgrade strategy for your house. It is essential to ensure that moisture does not enter the insulation or building envelope when insulating, installing, or upgrading the air barrier system. Comprehensive air leakage control involves the systematic identification and sealing of as many air leakage paths as possible using weatherstripping, caulking, gaskets, and tapes. Potential leakage areas include windows, doors, wall cracks, interior trim, and even ceiling light fixtures.

### Contracting the work

Homeowners can perform air sealing, but professional air sealers often achieve better results due to their experience. They may use tools like a depressurizing fan door (blower door) with smoke pencils to locate leaks and specialty caulking and sealants. Infrared scanners, combined with a blower door test, are powerful for identifying air leaks and missing insulation. Many sealing companies also offer testing for ventilation and combustion air requirements, including backdrafting of combustion gases.

### Effect on a house as a whole

As a house's envelope becomes tighter, household humidity levels can rise, potentially causing condensation and moisture problems. Additionally, reduced fresh air circulation can impact combustion appliances. Therefore, comprehensive air leakage control must include attention to controllable whole-house ventilation and combustion-air supply. Refer to Part 9.4, \"Ventilation and combustion air,\" for guidance on controlling ventilation and Part 2.4.2, \"How much humidity?\", and Figure 2-11, \"Sterling Chart,\" for indoor relative humidity.

Each house responds uniquely to air sealing, making monitoring crucial. Older homes may need remedial actions, such as mold cleanup and material replacement, before air sealing. Understanding how problems like moisture accumulation and mold buildup occur, and taking steps to control humidity and ventilation, is key to avoiding them.

Fuel-burning appliances (furnaces, water heaters, fireplaces, woodstoves) require air for combustion and to exhaust gases. Insufficient air can lead to dangerous gases spilling into the house. Humidity, ventilation, and combustion air are discussed further in Section 9, \"Operating your house.\"

## 4.1 Finding leakage areas

Air leaks occur where there is a hole in the building envelope and a pressure difference. In winter, houses act like chimneys due to the stack effect, with air entering at lower levels and exiting at upper levels and the ceiling.

### 4.1.1 How to locate air leaks

For optimal results, consider hiring an air sealing contractor or professional energy advisor. However, you can also perform a DIY check:

*   **Leak detector**: Use burning incense sticks (two or three together for more smoke). Powerful leaks will dissipate the smoke, while slower leaks will cause smoke to trail or move towards them.
*   **Windy/Cold Day Check**: On a cold, windy day, check for drafts in suspected areas.
*   **Interior Checks**: Look for leaks on interior walls and features like electrical outlets and switches, as these can provide paths to the outside.
*   **Rudimentary Fan Test**: Close all windows and doors. Turn on all exhaust appliances (bathroom/kitchen fans, clothes dryers on cool cycle, portable fans in windows). **Before starting, turn off any fuel-fired space and water heaters to prevent backdrafting of combustion gases.** Then, use your leak detector to identify and mark air leakage locations.

### 4.1.2 Checklist of air leakage areas

**Inside the main living areas, check the following:**

-   Window glazings for tightness and around the window sash and casing.
-   Around the door, including the threshold and around the door frame.
-   Electrical outlets and switches, including those on interior walls.
-   Exhaust fans and vents (should vent outside and close properly when not in use).
-   Corners where two walls meet with an imperfect seal.
-   Light fixtures in the ceiling.
-   Interior trim and baseboards.
-   Cracks in the wall finish or ceiling.
-   The joint where a wood frame wall joins a masonry wall or chimney.
-   Doors and hatches into unheated attics.
-   Fireplace dampers and fireplace bricks.
-   Behind bathtubs and under sinks.
-   Above sliding pocket doors.
-   Around plumbing pipes and ductwork.

**Inside the attic, check the following (you may need to move existing insulation):**

-   Around the plumbing stack and any other pipes entering the attic.
-   Around wires or ceiling light fixtures that penetrate the attic floor.
-   Around ducting that enters the attic from inside the house.
-   At the junction of the ceiling with interior wall partitions.
-   Around attic access doors.
-   Around chimneys.
-   Along any shared walls.
-   The ceiling area in bathrooms and above stairwells.

**Inside the basement, check the following:**

-   Where the wood-frame wall (sill plate) meets the masonry (concrete or stone) foundation or where joists penetrate the masonry wall.
-   Holes or gaps where the electrical service, gas service, or oil fill pipes go through the wall; **be careful around electrical wires and gas pipes; do not disturb connections.**
-   Holes for wiring, cabling, plumbing, and air conditioning pipes going into internal and external walls.
-   Leaky ducting or poorly fitted hot air registers or cold air intakes.
-   Around window and door framing.
-   Cracks in the foundation wall and slab.
-   Floor drains.
-   The base of the chimney or flue.

_**Safety warning:** See Part 1.4, \"Health and safety considerations,\" for a warning about asbestos and vermiculite insulation._

## 4.2 Caulking and other air sealing materials

Seal cracks and penetrations on the inside surface of exterior walls, ceilings, or floors to prevent air from escaping into the walls and roof. On the exterior, caulk cracks to prevent water and insect entry. If painting, avoid plugging siding joints and use permeable paint or stain, as the outside walls must be able to dispel moisture. Do not paint stucco; use special sealers instead.

### 4.2.1 Caulking basics

-   **Caulking Gun**: Purchase a good quality caulking gun that fits your hand, has a pressure release lever (thumb release is convenient), and ideally a nozzle cutter and tube piercer.
-   **Sealant Choice**: Choose the right sealant for the job. Use premium caulks for durability. Practice applying and smoothing beads before starting on the actual work.
-   **Application Guidelines (for polyethylene and vapour barriers, illustrated in Figure 4-3)**:
    -   Use wide sheets to minimize seams.
    -   Overlap all seams and edges over a solid backing (e.g., a stud).
    -   Apply a bead of non-hardening acoustical sealant between overlapped sheets over the support.
    -   Staple through the sheets and the sealant bead. Minimize other staples.
    -   The finish (e.g., drywall) anchors the seam. If polyethylene is recessed, a batten nailed over the seam can provide mechanical support.
    -   Seal all penetrations. Where possible, penetrate at a solid backing (e.g., plywood) or drill through a single or double stud.

_**Safety warning:** Use non-combustible caulking around heat sources (chimney, light fixtures, fan motors, etc.). Silicone or polysulphide sealants work well; special high-temperature silicones are available for flue pipes. Always check product labels._

### 4.2.2 Other sealing materials and applications

Other materials used for air barriers include specialty gaskets and tapes, as well as sheet materials like polyethylene, spun-bonded olefin, rigid insulation, drywall, plywood, Oriented Strand Board (OSB), and sheet metal. Correct installation is crucial for sheet materials; seal all edges, seams, and penetrations.

### 4.2.3 Air and vapour barriers

A new air and vapour barrier can often be installed by using sealed drywall as the air barrier and layers of paint or sheet polyethylene as the vapour barrier. Alternatively, sealed sheet polyethylene installed on the warm side of the insulation can serve as both. Proper installation is critical (see Figure 4-3).

### 4.2.4 Refinishing the interior

If renovating the interior along with an attic retrofit, consider removing the ceiling and installing a new sheet-polyethylene or sealed drywall air barrier on the underside of the ceiling joists. Sealing the air barrier on the ceiling to the wall should be straightforward, but maintaining continuity at interior partitions requires careful, detailed work. For partition walls perpendicular to ceiling joists, ensure continuity by working from above with connecting strips of polyethylene or extruded polystyrene. For parallel partition walls, install blocking and nailing strips for support.

### 4.2.5 Tips on sealing some of the leakiest areas

#### Electrical outlets

Seal any electrical outlets with drafts, including interior wall outlets. **Always turn off the power to the outlet first.** For a better seal, caulk the gasket before installation. Special approved foam pads fit between the cover plate and receptacles. Child safety plugs with gaskets can be used for seldom-used outlets. During renovations, use special plastic boxes for optimal seals, caulking the wire penetration and sealing the air and vapour barrier to the box edge.

#### Trim areas

Seal air leakage areas around all baseboards, moldings, and window/door casings. This can be done by sealing joints with a flexible caulk (clear, paintable, or matching color). A more effective method is to carefully remove the trim, fill wide cracks with a foam backer rod, and seal with caulking or appropriate polyurethane foam (see Figure 4-5). If baseboards are removed, caulk between the wall finish and bottom wall plates, and between plates and the floor.

#### Glass panes (glazings)

Ensure the seal between glass and its wood frame is tight. Check glazing for cracks or missing sections. Repair with glazing compound, which lasts longer and remains semi-soft longer than putty. Remove old putty, apply new material with a putty knife, press firmly for a good seal, and then paint, allowing the paint to just touch the glass.

#### Fireplaces

Close the chimney damper when not in use and ensure it closes fully (repair if not). Even with a closed damper, heat can escape. Consider installing tight-fitting glass doors; ensure the door frame is tightly sealed to the fireplace with non-combustible caulk and includes a combustion air gate or damper. The air gate should be closed when not in use. For unused or seasonally used fireplaces, create an airtight plug for the chimney or opening, made from cloth-covered board material that seals well at the edges (see Figure 4-6). Check and caulk the joint where the chimney meets the wall with high-temperature caulking.

#### Chimney

Chimneys present unique challenges for air-sealing and insulation.

#### Attic hatch

Seal the attic hatch like an exterior door. Caulk around the frame and between the casing and ceiling drywall. Weatherstrip the edges of the casing or access panel. Install hooks or latches to hold the hatch firmly against the weatherstripping (see Figure 4-7). The hatch itself should be insulated.

_**Safety warning:** Fire safety is a significant concern during air-sealing and insulating. Refer to Part 5.1.2, \"Fire and other hazards in attics,\" for relevant methods._

#### Windows that are never opened

For windows not needed for ventilation or emergency escape, use strippable caulk instead of weatherstripping. This allows for removal if the window needs to be operated again.
